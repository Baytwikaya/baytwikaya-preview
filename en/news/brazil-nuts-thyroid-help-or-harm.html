Brazil nuts and the thyroid: do they help or harm?

Share this article:

One nut can cover the whole day's need… so where is the problem?

A claim has spread on social media that Brazil nuts “treat” the thyroid, because a single nut can contain more selenium than a person needs in a whole day. An investigation published by Al Jazeera on 26 November 2026 examined this claim and reviewed the scientific evidence behind it — and the conclusion was far more nuanced than the circulating headlines: selenium is genuinely essential for the thyroid, but the nut is not a treatment, the amount that may help is small, and the amount that may harm is not far from it.

According to the US National Institutes of Health, one ounce of Brazil nuts (about 28 grams, or 6 to 8 nuts) contains 544 micrograms of selenium — about 68 to 91 micrograms per nut — while the daily need for an adult is only 55 micrograms. But the problem is that the content of a single nut is not fixed at all, as we shall see.

Why does the body need selenium?

Selenium is a trace mineral built into enzymes that convert the thyroid hormone T4 into its active form T3, and into antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase that protect thyroid cells. The thyroid holds a relatively high concentration of selenium, and its function can be disturbed by deficiency, especially combined with iodine deficiency. But not every thyroid patient is selenium-deficient: deficiency is rare in the United States and Canada, and the risk groups are mainly dialysis patients, people living with HIV, and people who depend on local food grown in low-selenium soil.

Here lies the key point: correcting a real deficiency may help, but adding more when levels are already adequate has no proven benefit and may carry unnecessary risk. Selenium is not a vitamin where “more is better” — it is a mineral with a narrow window between enough and too much.

What do the studies say about Hashimoto's and hypothyroidism?

The only study that tested Brazil nuts themselves on thyroid hormones was a small pilot from 2015: 40 dialysis patients with low selenium and low T3 ate one nut a day for 3 months. Blood selenium rose from 17.6 to 153.4 micrograms per litre, and T3 rose from 27.3 to 50.2 nanograms per decilitre but stayed below normal, while thyroid-stimulating hormone did not change significantly. It was a small study with no control group, in dialysis patients only — not proof of a treatment for hypothyroidism in general.

As for selenium supplement studies in Hashimoto's disease, they showed modest laboratory results that do not translate into symptom improvement: a 2024 meta-analysis of 35 trials found a fall in thyroid-stimulating hormone only in patients not treated with thyroid hormone, and a fall in antibodies, with no change in T4 and T3. And in the CATALYST trial of 412 patients, half took 200 micrograms of selenium a day for 12 months alongside levothyroxine: quality of life improved equally in both groups, antibodies fell in the selenium group, but the levothyroxine dose did not change. A change in laboratory numbers has not been shown to change the course of the disease or replace the medicine.

When does selenium become dangerous?

The tolerable upper limit for adults is 400 micrograms a day in the United States and 255 micrograms a day in the European Union, after the European Food Safety Authority lowered it in 2023 from 300. Symptoms of selenium poisoning include garlic-smelling breath, a metallic taste, hair loss, brittle nails, nausea, diarrhoea, extreme tiredness and nervous system problems; very high intakes can cause severe stomach and nervous system problems, breathing difficulty, tremor and kidney or heart failure, and rarely death. The risk is one of chronic overconsumption, not of a single nut.

But the arithmetic shows how narrow the margin is: using the typical figures (68 to 91 micrograms per nut), about 3 to 4 nuts reach the European limit and about 4 to 6 reach the American limit. A single standard serving (6 to 8 nuts, 544 micrograms) exceeds the American limit on its own, before any other food or supplement. The nuts in the 2015 study contained about 290 micrograms per nut, so less than one nut was enough to reach the European limit. Most strikingly, a global review published in November 2026 found that selenium in Brazil nuts ranges from 0.5 to 252.3 micrograms per gram — meaning one nut could contain anywhere from about 2.5 to about 1,260 micrograms — so no fixed “safe number” can be guaranteed.

The one medically recognised exception

There is one use of selenium recognised in thyroid guidelines: a 200-microgram daily dose for 6 months for mild, active Graves' orbitopathy — an eye complication — based on a trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2011 in 159 patients, which found improved quality of life and slower disease progression. This is a defined supplement course for an eye complication, not a treatment for the thyroid itself, not for moderate or severe disease, and not a reason to eat Brazil nuts.

As for a reasonable amount as food, one to two nuts a day is the commonly cited ceiling, and the 2026 global review estimated that 2 to 10 nuts a week are enough to supplement a low-selenium diet, depending on region and origin. Do not combine daily Brazil nuts with a selenium supplement or a multivitamin containing selenium without counting the total. Particular care is needed for thyroid patients taking levothyroxine, pregnant and breastfeeding women, children (whose limits are much lower), and people with kidney disease.

Tip

Do not treat thyroid disease with Brazil nuts, and never stop levothyroxine for nuts or a selenium supplement. As food, one to two nuts a day at most is enough — not a handful every day. If you suspect a selenium deficiency, ask your doctor for a blood test before any therapeutic dose: an unmeasurable dose in a nut of unpredictable content makes a poor medicine.

The bottom line: a useful food in small amounts… not a thyroid medicine

Al Jazeera's 2026 investigation concludes that there is not enough clinical evidence to recommend Brazil nuts as a treatment for hypothyroidism or Hashimoto's. Selenium is essential for converting T4 to T3 and protecting thyroid cells, and correcting a real deficiency may help — but adding more when levels are adequate has no proven benefit. A single nut may contain anywhere from about 2.5 to about 1,260 micrograms, so there is no fixed safe number. The reasonable amount is one to two nuts a day at most, or a few nuts a week, without stopping medication and without combining them with a selenium supplement.

  • Need and limit: the adult daily need is 55 micrograms (60 in pregnancy, 70 when breastfeeding); the tolerable upper limit is 400 micrograms a day in the United States and 255 micrograms in the European Union (lowered in 2023 from 300).
