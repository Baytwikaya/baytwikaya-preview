After a heart attack, a flu shot nearly halves the risk of death

Share this article:

An ordinary vaccine after a heart attack… and a result that surprises doctors

Flu is not just a bad cold for someone who has had a heart attack. The infection can trigger acute cardiovascular events, and the inflammation that comes with it strains a heart that is already weakened. That is why international guidelines recommend an annual flu vaccine for heart patients — and giving it during the hospital stay after a heart attack carries the highest class of recommendation in international guidance.

But a new Swedish study, published on 20 November 2026 in The Lancet Regional Health — Europe, reveals a wide gap between the recommendation and reality: of more than 12,000 eligible patients, only 6% received the vaccine in hospital. Even so, the difference in deaths over the following year was clear — and close to half.

What did the Swedish study do?

The study was led by Ole Frøbert, consultant cardiologist at Örebro University Hospital and professor at Aarhus University, with colleagues from several Swedish hospitals. The team used SWEDEHEART, Sweden’s national cardiac care quality registry, and followed patients admitted to hospital with a heart attack over four flu seasons, from 2021–22 to 2024–25, excluding those who had already received that season’s flu vaccine before admission.

There were 12,711 eligible patients: 760 (6.0%) received a flu vaccine during their hospital stay, while 11,951 (94.0%) did not. The researchers then matched 723 vaccinated patients with 723 unvaccinated patients who resembled them in their characteristics, followed them for 12 months, and took all-cause mortality over one year as the main outcome.

The key number: deaths fall from 4.9% to 2.7%

Over 12 months, 18 vaccinated patients died compared with 33 unvaccinated patients — estimated mortality rates of 2.7% versus 4.9%, meaning the risk of death fell by nearly half. After statistical adjustment, the hazard ratio was 0.53, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.29 to 0.98 and a p-value of 0.042, so the result is statistically significant. Before matching, the reduction looked even larger: a hazard ratio of 0.35.

But the finding does not cover everything: the researchers found no significant difference in readmission for a heart attack (hazard ratio 1.08), nor in non-staged coronary interventions (0.89), nor in major adverse cardiac events combined (0.84). In other words, the vaccine’s effect showed up in mortality specifically, not in every measure.

The paradox: only 6% received the vaccine

Although the international randomised IAMI trial — led by Frøbert himself and published in Circulation in 2021 in 2,571 patients — showed that vaccination after a heart attack cut all-cause mortality by 41% (2.9% versus 4.9%, hazard ratio 0.59), and that the combined events fell from 7.2% to 5.3% (hazard ratio 0.72), and although the guidelines gave the vaccine the highest class of recommendation, uptake in routine care remained low.

The researchers wrote that “adherence remained strikingly low”, and that patients may underestimate the value of something routine that seems unrelated to heart disease, such as a flu shot. Cardiology wards themselves may also fail to check vaccination status on admission, or to offer the vaccine before discharge. The researchers therefore call for a clearer routine: check vaccination status on admission, offer the vaccine to every eligible patient before they leave hospital, and then vaccinate annually.

Read the result carefully: what did the study not prove?

The study is observational, not a randomised trial: it records an association between in-hospital vaccination and lower mortality, and does not prove that the vaccine alone is the cause. Patients who accept the vaccine may differ from others in frailty, social circumstances and health behaviours — the so-called healthy-vaccinee bias — although the statistical matching tried to reduce that effect.

Some vaccinations given outside the hospital may also not have been recorded, and no cause-of-death data were available, so we do not know whether the reduction concerns cardiovascular deaths specifically. Even so, the consistency of the finding with the randomised IAMI trial strengthens confidence in it, and makes the practical message clear: the in-hospital vaccine is a simple step — but one that is rarely taken.

Tip

If you have had a heart attack or live with heart disease, ask your doctor about the flu vaccine before leaving hospital, and repeat it every year during flu season — it is among the highest-grade recommendations in cardiology guidance. Do not postpone the vaccine because you “feel better”: flu itself can trigger a new cardiac event. And if you missed the vaccine in hospital, ask for it at your first medical visit after discharge.

The bottom line: a vaccine in hospital before discharge may save a life

A Swedish study of 12,711 patients found that an in-hospital flu vaccine after a heart attack was linked to a fall in one-year mortality from 4.9% to 2.7%, with a hazard ratio of 0.53, consistent with the randomised IAMI trial. But only 6% of eligible patients received the vaccine, despite the highest class of recommendation. The message is not the discovery of a new drug, but the implementation of what we already know: check vaccination status on admission, and offer the vaccine before discharge.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the studies

  • Publication: The Lancet Regional Health — Europe, 20 November 2026; DOI: 10.1016/j.lanepe.2026.101922; first author Ole Frøbert, Örebro University Hospital, Sweden.
  • Registry: SWEDEHEART, Sweden’s national cardiac care quality registry; four flu seasons from 2021–22 to 2024–25.
  • Patients: 12,711 eligible patients; 760 (6.0%) received the vaccine in hospital and 11,951 (94.0%) did not; 723 vaccinated patients were matched with 723 unvaccinated patients.
  • Deaths over 12 months: 2.7% in vaccinated patients versus 4.9% in unvaccinated patients — 18 deaths versus 33; hazard ratio 0.53 (95% CI: 0.29–0.98, p-value 0.042); before matching, hazard ratio 0.35.
  • No significant difference: readmission for a heart attack (hazard ratio 1.08), non-staged coronary interventions (0.89), and major adverse cardiac events combined (0.84).
  • The IAMI trial (Circulation 2021, 2,571 patients): all-cause mortality 2.9% versus 4.9% (hazard ratio 0.59, number needed to treat 50), cardiovascular mortality 2.7% versus 4.5%, and combined events 5.3% versus 7.2% (hazard ratio 0.72); a Class I recommendation.
  • Limitations: an observational study (association, not causation); healthy-vaccinee bias; vaccinations outside hospital may not have been recorded; and no cause-of-death data.
