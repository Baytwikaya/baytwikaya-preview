Air-pollution metals trigger asthma through the “CaSR” receptor… and a new preventive treatment is on the horizon

Share this article:

Why does air pollution trigger asthma? Now we know the mechanism

Asthma patients know well that days of heavy pollution bring worse attacks, but the question that remained unanswered was: how exactly do polluting particles trigger inflammation in the lung? A new study published in the journal eBioMedicine on 24 November 2026 offers the first complete mechanistic answer: the metals in pollution particles activate a receptor in airway cells called the calcium-sensing receptor, or CaSR, setting off an inflammatory chain that ends in an asthma attack. The study was led by Chris Corrigan of King’s College London and Bethan Mansfield of Cardiff University, in collaboration with Imperial College London and Queen Mary University of London.

CaSR is a receptor already known for its role in monitoring blood calcium levels and in the parathyroid glands, but this study found it strongly expressed in airway lining cells and innate immune cells in the lung. When the positively charged metals in the particles reach it, the receptor assumes the calcium level is high and releases inflammatory signals. Pollution thus changes from a vague “irritation” into a specific chemical signal.

Evidence from the real world: calcium in the air and more attacks in 71,000 patients

The study did not stop at laboratory experiments; it linked them to real-world data: the researchers examined a population sample of about 71,000 patients in East London who had oral corticosteroid prescriptions for asthma attacks, and compared the level of calcium in airborne particles in the preceding week with asthma attacks that required extra corticosteroid doses. They found that higher calcium in the air was associated with more attacks requiring corticosteroids.

Calcium is not the only metal that activates the receptor: other positively charged metals in the particles, such as strontium, cadmium and nickel, also activate CaSR. This means that the “cocktail” of metals in polluted city air may act through one shared receptor, which explains why pollution is linked to asthma attacks even on days when the air does not look heavily polluted.

In the laboratory: switching the receptor off stopped the inflammation

The team tested the mechanism step by step: in HEK293 cells, the particles activated the CaSR receptor directly; in human bronchial lining cells, the particles released inflammatory alarm signals and inflammatory mediators; in immune cells, they caused dendritic cells to mature, as shown by a rise in the CD83 marker. Then came the decisive test: when negative allosteric modulators of the CaSR receptor — molecules that dampen the receptor’s activity — were added, all of these responses stopped.

And in a mouse model, a topically applied negative modulator of the receptor significantly stopped the lung inflammation and remodelling driven by interleukin-33, one of the main alarm signals in asthma. This is the basis on which the researchers propose a preventive treatment idea: an inhaled topical negative modulator could prevent severe asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease from worsening before it starts, instead of waiting for the attack and treating it.

What does this mean for asthma patients today?

First, this is a proof-of-concept study, not a treatment: there is no CaSR-based preventive drug available to patients, no human clinical trial yet, and the road from mice to the clinic is long. The news does not change your treatment today; it explains why pollution triggers your attacks and opens a door to a future preventive treatment.

Second, the practical message today is the same one asthma patients already know: on days of heavy pollution, reduce your exposure to outdoor air as much as possible, follow the air-quality index in your city, always carry your rescue inhaler with you, stick to the preventive treatment plan your doctor prescribed, and do not stop your inhaled corticosteroid on your own. The new mechanism explains the danger, but protection today still lies in these simple measures.

Tip

On days of heavy pollution, check your city’s air-quality index before going out, reduce physical activity outdoors, close the windows, carry your rescue inhaler with you, and stick to your preventive treatment. If you notice your attacks increasing with pollution despite sticking to the treatment, tell your doctor — your plan may need reviewing. The new preventive treatment is still an early idea, so do not wait for a drug soon.

The bottom line: one receptor turns metals into an attack… and a preventive treatment on the horizon

The 2026 King’s College London and Cardiff study in eBioMedicine identified for the first time the mechanism by which air pollution triggers asthma: particle metals, led by calcium, activate the CaSR receptor in airway and immune cells, releasing inflammatory signals; in a sample of about 71,000 patients in East London, calcium in the air was linked to more attacks requiring corticosteroids; in the laboratory, negative modulators of the receptor stopped all responses, and in mice a topical modulator stopped the inflammation and remodelling driven by interleukin-33. The idea is a future topical preventive treatment, not an available drug.
