Vitamin D deficiency is linked to about 23 minutes less sleep a night

Share this article:

23 minutes less every night… what links vitamin D to sleep?

Can a vitamin known for bones and immunity also affect how long you sleep? That is what a new study from Johns Hopkins University examined, published in The Journals of Gerontology in November 2026. It followed 460 adults aged 40 and over, with a mean age of about 71 years, 52.8% of them women, all without cognitive impairment, within the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging between 2012 and 2019.

The headline result: those whose vitamin D levels were deficient slept about 23 minutes less per night, on average, than those whose levels were normal. And those in the top third of vitamin D levels slept about 20 minutes longer than those in the bottom third. Average sleep in the sample was about 6.7 hours a night.

How did the researchers measure sleep and vitamin D?

The researchers did not rely on asking people about their sleep. They used a wrist-worn sensor measuring movement and light for up to seven nights — an objective measurement that does not depend on memory, though it cannot distinguish sleep stages. They measured total sleep time, sleep efficiency, sleep onset latency, wake after sleep onset, and the average length of each night-time waking. Vitamin D was measured with a fasting blood test for 25-hydroxyvitamin D.

They classified levels as follows: deficiency below 20 nanograms per millilitre, insufficiency between 20 and 30, and normal above 30. About 30% of participants were below 30 nanograms per millilitre. But note: these cut-offs are not universal. The US National Academies consider below 12 nanograms per millilitre a deficiency risk, 12 to below 20 inadequate, and 20 or more adequate for most people. The analysis adjusted for age, sex, race, education, number of illnesses, depressive symptoms and body weight.

Longer sleep… and less broken sleep

The difference was not only in sleep duration. As vitamin D rose, each night-time waking spell was about 17 seconds shorter on average, and sleep efficiency was about 1.4 percentage points higher in the top third than in the bottom third — meaning more of the time in bed was actually spent asleep. The published abstract reported no significant vitamin D result for sleep onset latency or for total wake time during the night.

The association appeared strongest among Black participants, in an exploratory analysis, noting that Black adults in the United States have a higher prevalence of both vitamin D deficiency and sleep disturbance. Vitamin B12, also measured in the study, showed no clear link with any sleep measure, and the findings about it were inconclusive — especially since only 14 participants (3%) were low in B12.

Does this mean a vitamin D supplement improves sleep?

The short answer: no, not from this study. The study was observational and cross-sectional: it measured vitamin D and sleep at the same time, gave nobody a supplement, and cannot prove that low vitamin D causes short sleep — or the reverse. It is possible that short sleep or declining health reduces time outdoors, which lowers vitamin D. Vitamin D may simply be a marker of time outdoors, physical activity or general health, rather than a direct cause.

What we know about the supplements themselves is limited: a 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis found that vitamin D supplements improved perceived sleep quality (the Pittsburgh scale), but their effects on sleep quantity were not agreed upon and need further research. The Johns Hopkins researchers note that earlier supplement trials were small and homogeneous, with some suggesting benefit. Supplements are therefore not presented here as a proven way to lengthen sleep.

What should you do in practice?

The body makes most vitamin D from sunlight on the skin. Food sources include oily fish such as salmon and sardines, red meat, egg yolks, liver and fortified foods. Food alone is usually not enough to meet needs. The recommended daily intake in the United States is 600 international units for adults aged 19 to 70, and 800 units for those over 71. Vitamin D status is measured with a blood test for 25-hydroxyvitamin D, and the US Endocrine Society does not recommend routine testing in healthy people.

The wider context explains why the topic matters: vitamin D deficiency is very common worldwide. A meta-analysis of 7.9 million participants found that 47.9% had levels below 20 nanograms per millilitre, and the figure in the Eastern Mediterranean region, which includes Morocco, reached 71.8%. Meanwhile, about 30.5% of American adults sleep less than 7 hours a night. Both problems are common, and the new study links them — but it does not prove that one causes the other.

Tip

Do not start high doses of vitamin D on your own in order to sleep longer: there is no evidence that they lengthen sleep, and excess supplements can cause harm. Rely on sunlight and food first, and if your sleep stays short or broken for weeks, see your doctor — the cause could be sleep apnoea or something else that needs diagnosis. If your doctor advises a vitamin D test, it is a simple blood test.

The bottom line: a real but modest link… not a supplement prescription

The 2026 Johns Hopkins study of 460 adults averaging 71 years found that those deficient in vitamin D slept about 23 minutes less per night than those with normal levels, that the top third slept about 20 minutes longer than the bottom third, with slightly shorter waking spells and slightly higher sleep efficiency. But the study was observational and did not test supplements; it does not prove that vitamin D deficiency causes short sleep, or that a supplement lengthens it. The message is to look after sunlight and food, and to see a doctor if the sleep problem persists.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the studies

  • Publication: Chen et al., The Journals of Gerontology: Series A 2026, vol. 81, issue 11, article glag212; published online 14 October 2026; DOI: 10.1093/gerona/glag212; first author Diefei Chen, senior author Adam Spira, Johns Hopkins University; funded by the US National Institute on Aging.
  • Design and sample: serial cross-sectional analysis within the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging; 460 men and women aged 40 and over, mean age 70.9 years, 52.8% women, 22.2% Black, cognitively unimpaired; assessed between 2012 and 2019.
  • Sleep duration: those who were deficient slept about 23 minutes less per night than those with normal levels (coefficient −23.03 minutes, 95% CI: −42.05 to −4.01); the top third slept about 20 minutes longer than the bottom third; mean sleep in the sample was about 6.7 hours a night.
  • Broken sleep: as vitamin D rose, each night-time waking spell was about 17 seconds shorter on average, and sleep efficiency was about 1.4 percentage points higher in the top third than in the bottom third.
  • Measurement: vitamin D by fasting blood test for 25-hydroxyvitamin D; sleep by wrist device for up to 7 nights (objective measurement that does not distinguish sleep stages); about 30% of participants were below 30 nanograms per millilitre.
  • Thresholds: the study counted deficiency as below 20 nanograms per millilitre and normal as above 30; the US National Academies instead count below 12 as deficiency risk and 20 or more as adequate for most people.
  • Limitations: observational cross-sectional study — association, not causation; no supplement was tested; about 74% of participants had measurements from a single visit only; relatively healthy volunteers averaging 71 years.
  • Context: worldwide, 47.9% of people are below 20 nanograms per millilitre, and in the Eastern Mediterranean region 71.8%; about 30.5% of American adults sleep less than 7 hours a night.
