Blood pressure starts to diverge 30 years before a heart attack or stroke

Share this article:

Thirty years before the event… your body was already sending the signal

When does the risk of a heart attack or stroke begin? The new answer from a large study published in JAMA Network Open on 23 November 2026 may surprise you: the trajectories of risk factors start to diverge decades, not just a few years, before the event. People who later had a heart attack or stroke, or died of a cardiac cause, already had higher blood pressure readings and different blood markers from the others, starting about 30 years before the event.

The study was led by Marie de Bakker of the University of Edinburgh and drew on data from 4 long-running cohort studies in the United States, Norway and the United Kingdom, collected between 1985 and 2019. The researchers followed 113,612 adults who had never had a heart attack or stroke, across 316,719 study visits, with a median follow-up of about 18.9 years. The event they tracked was a first nonfatal heart attack, a first nonfatal stroke, or cardiovascular death, which occurred in 8,025 women (13.5%) and 8,892 men (16.4%).

Blood pressure: a small gap at first… then it widens

The most striking figure in the study concerns systolic blood pressure, the higher number in a reading. Three decades before the event, average systolic blood pressure in women who later had an event was 4 mm Hg higher than in women who did not (121 versus 117 mm Hg), and by the time of the event or the end of follow-up the gap had widened to 7 mm Hg (137 versus 130). Similar long-term differences appeared in men too.

What is striking is that the gap starts while the values are still within what is considered normal: 121 mm Hg is an entirely ordinary reading, yet it already carried a signal. The researchers also found a difference between the sexes with age: at age 20, average systolic blood pressure was 110 mm Hg in women versus 123 in men, but women’s pressure rises more steeply with age and overtakes men’s at a mean age of 66 years. This is a reminder that a “normal” reading does not necessarily mean a reading identical to healthy people over the long term.

Not blood pressure alone: blood markers lead the way too

The early divergence was not limited to blood pressure. Differences in cardiac troponin I, a protein that signals strain on the heart muscle, began to appear 3 decades before the event in both women and men. For C-reactive protein, a marker of inflammation, the difference appeared 3 decades ahead in women and 2 decades ahead in men. The difference in N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide, a marker used in monitoring heart failure, appeared about 1 decade before the event in both sexes.

By the time of the event, those affected were also more likely to carry other risk factors: diabetes was present in 15.3% of the women who had an event versus 9.3% of those who did not, and in 15.2% of the men who had an event versus 9.0% of those who did not; current smoking was present in 16.6% of the women who had an event versus only 3.6% of those who did not, and in 14.7% of the men who had an event versus 2.7% of those who did not. The picture is not a single factor, but a build-up of exposure stretching over decades.

What does this mean for you? Prevention starts very early

The practical message of this study is not to worry about a single reading, but to understand that preventing heart disease is a path that runs across the whole of life. Small differences in blood pressure, even within the normal range, accumulate over decades, and early action — regular movement, a balanced diet, quitting smoking, and treating blood pressure, diabetes and cholesterol when needed — is what changes the trajectory before the gap widens.

It is important to read the study with its limits in mind: it is observational, so an association does not prove that higher blood pressure alone is the cause; the trajectory estimates were also restricted to people not taking blood pressure or cholesterol medicines, which may underestimate how risk progresses in the highest-risk people; and ethnicity data were limited in some cohorts, so trajectories could not be compared across ethnic groups. Even so, the size of the sample and the length of follow-up make the signal strong: prevention does not start at fifty, it starts in youth.

Tip

Measure your blood pressure regularly from a young age, even if you feel healthy, and keep a record of your readings to review with your doctor. It is the small difference that persists for years that builds the risk later, and daily movement, less salt, quitting smoking, and treating blood pressure when needed are the proven ways to change the trajectory before it widens.

The bottom line: a signal 30 years ahead… and prevention is a lifelong path

The 2026 University of Edinburgh study in JAMA Network Open found that the trajectories of blood pressure, risk factors and biomarkers diverge between people who will have a heart attack or stroke and those who will not, up to 3 decades before the event. Systolic blood pressure in women was 4 mm Hg higher 3 decades ahead, and 7 mm Hg higher at the event; troponin differences began 3 decades ahead, C-reactive protein 3 decades ahead in women and 2 in men, and natriuretic peptide 1 decade ahead. The message: watch your blood pressure from youth, and act early, because it is the small accumulated differences that build the risk.
