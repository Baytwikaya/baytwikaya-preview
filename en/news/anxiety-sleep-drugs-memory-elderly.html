Medicines that cloud thinking… still prescribed to older adults

There are medicines doctors have known for years can weaken memory and thinking in older people, especially those with cognitive disorders such as dementia and mild cognitive impairment. Yet a new study from the University at Buffalo, published in the Journal of the American Geriatrics Society in October 2026, found these medicines are still prescribed to about one in three older adults with cognitive disorders in the United States.

The team, led by Haowen Hsu and David Jacobs, followed data from the US Medical Expenditure Panel Survey between 2011 and 2022, covering 2,796 people aged 65 and over with cognitive disorders living at home, representing about 29.7 million people. The question was not “do these medicines harm memory?” but “how many people still receive them, and what outcomes are linked to them?”

Which medicines are meant? Four groups

The study speaks of what are called “cognitive potentially inappropriate medications”: medicines classified under the American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria. They cover four groups: benzodiazepines such as alprazolam, diazepam and lorazepam, used for anxiety and insomnia; non-benzodiazepine hypnotics, or “Z-drugs”, such as zolpidem, zaleplon and eszopiclone, used for insomnia; anticholinergics such as some older antidepressants, bladder drugs and sedating antihistamines; and antipsychotics, used for agitation and psychosis, including the behavioural symptoms of dementia.

The Beers Criteria, in their 2023 update covering around 100 medicines that may be inappropriate for people over 65, strongly recommend avoiding benzodiazepines and Z-drugs in older adults, especially for insomnia, and avoiding antipsychotics, anticholinergics, benzodiazepines and Z-drugs in people with dementia, because they are linked to cognitive impairment, delirium, falls, fractures and hospitalisation, with only narrow exceptions. It is worth noting that the study did not include gabapentinoids or antidepressants in general in these groups.

The numbers: slow improvement, and a price still being paid

The good news is that the share of people receiving these medicines fell: from 39.3% in 2011 to 29.3% in 2022, an annual fall of 1.9%. But nearly one in three still received them in 2022 — a large figure for the very patients most exposed to their harm. The study did not examine why the share fell; it only recorded the fall.

As for the price linked to them, it was clear in the numbers: those who received these medicines had 72% higher odds of poor mental quality of life (odds ratio 1.72), a 41% higher rate of emergency visits, and a 38% higher rate of hospital stays. The researchers found no significant link with physical quality of life or with outpatient visits. These are associations, not proof that the medicines are the sole cause, because the patients who receive them may already be sicker.

What does this mean for your family?

If one of your older parents or relatives takes medicines for anxiety, sleep, the bladder or depression, the practical message of this study is simple: review the whole medicine list with the doctor and the pharmacist, and ask about each medicine: is it still needed? Is there a lower dose or a safer alternative? Bring to the appointment a complete list including over-the-counter sleep aids and antihistamines, and watch for any new confusion, drowsiness or falls.

It is worth noting that the research group is named after Alice Brennan, who died in 2009 six weeks after taking a muscle relaxant classified as inappropriate under the Beers Criteria; her daughter Mary told her story. Regular medicine review is not an administrative step — it can be a matter of safety. The study also notes its limits: it is observational and cross-sectional, it does not measure memory itself, it covered only people living at home, and it relied on households reporting the medicines.

Read the result carefully: what the study does not prove

It is important to correct the first impression a news headline may leave: this study did not measure memory decline over time, and it reported no rate of decline, no dementia risk figure, and no falls or mortality figure. The known harm of these medicines to cognition rests on their pharmacology and on the Beers Criteria, not on a direct measurement in this study. Likewise, the phrase “premature death” that appeared in some press coverage is general background, not a result of the study.

Most important of all: never stop a prescribed medicine suddenly. Stopping benzodiazepines and sleep medicines abruptly can cause rebound anxiety and insomnia, and in severe cases seizures, so any reduction must be gradual and supervised by a doctor. The first-line treatment for insomnia in older adults is not a medicine but cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia together with healthy sleep habits; and for anxiety, serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressants combined with psychotherapy are preferred over benzodiazepines.

Tip

If an older person in your family takes a medicine for anxiety or sleep, never stop it suddenly. Ask the doctor and the pharmacist to review the full medicine list: is every medicine still needed? Is there a lower dose or a safer alternative? Ask about cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia and healthy sleep habits before any sleeping pill, and watch for any new confusion, drowsiness or falls.

The bottom line: medicines known to endanger cognition are still common… and regular review is the answer

The 2026 University at Buffalo study found that the share of older adults with cognitive disorders receiving medicines that can harm cognition fell from 39.3% to 29.3% between 2011 and 2022, but still nears one in three. Receiving these medicines was linked to worse mental quality of life and more emergency visits and hospital stays. The study did not measure memory decline directly, but it is a reminder that these medicines are classed as inappropriate for older adults under the Beers Criteria, and that regular medicine review with the doctor and pharmacist, gradual reduction under supervision, and non-drug treatment first are the safe path.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the studies

  • Publication: Hsu et al., Journal of the American Geriatrics Society 2026, vol. 74, issue 10, pp. 2888–2897; published online 4 August 2026; DOI: 10.1111/jgs.70607; University at Buffalo; funded by the US Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality.
  • Design and sample: cross-sectional analysis of US Medical Expenditure Panel Survey data, 2011–2022; 2,796 people aged 65 and over with cognitive disorders living at home, representing about 29.7 million people.
  • Prevalence: the share receiving at least one of these medicines fell from 39.3% in 2011 to 29.3% in 2022, an annual fall of 1.9%.
  • Mental quality of life: 72% higher odds of poor mental quality of life among those who received these medicines (odds ratio 1.72, 95% CI: 1.31–2.25); no significant link with physical quality of life.
  • Healthcare use: 41% higher emergency visit rate (incidence rate ratio 1.41, CI 1.24–1.60) and 38% higher hospitalisation rate (1.38, CI 1.19–1.59); outpatient visits 27% higher without statistical significance (1.27, CI 0.75–1.54).
  • The four groups: benzodiazepines, Z-drug hypnotics, anticholinergics and antipsychotics — under the 2023 Beers Criteria. Gabapentinoids were not included in the definition.
  • What the study did not measure: no memory scores, no decline rate, no dementia risk figure and no falls or mortality figures; the study is observational and cross-sectional, and association does not mean causation.
  • Context: about 9% of adults over 65 in the United States use benzodiazepines each year, women at about twice the rate of men; about 44% of men and 57% of women over 65 take 5 or more medicines a week; and about 75% of them live with two or more chronic conditions.
