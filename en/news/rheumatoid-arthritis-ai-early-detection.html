Rheumatoid arthritis starts years before the pain: can AI detect it early?

Share this article:

The disease begins before you feel it

When a patient complains of joint pain, swelling and morning stiffness, rheumatoid arthritis has already come a long way. The new review published in Nature Reviews Rheumatology recalls a fact rheumatologists have known for years: the disease begins silently, years before the first pain, when the immune system starts attacking the body’s own tissues and antibodies such as rheumatoid factor and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies appear in the blood.

The new question the review raises — led by Michael Holers of the University of Colorado Anschutz with his colleagues — is: if the disease starts this early, can artificial intelligence spot those at risk before their joints are damaged? The answer, according to the researchers, is closer than it used to be, but it has not yet reached the doctor’s clinic.

What have recent studies found?

Since 2001, researchers at the University of Colorado have followed the SERA cohort — people at risk of rheumatoid arthritis because of a family history or antibodies in their blood. That follow-up showed that immune changes begin years before pain appears, and widen gradually: widespread inflammation, changes in immune cells, and disruption of the microbiome — the community of microbes in the mouth, gut and lungs.

And in a study published in 2025 in Science Translational Medicine, involving the Allen Institute, the University of Colorado Anschutz, the University of California San Diego and the Benaroya Research Institute, researchers found in carriers of anti-citrullinated peptide antibodies — before any swollen joints — widespread inflammation and changes in immune cells. In other words, the body sends early signals that can be measured, before the patient feels anything.

How could AI detect the risk early?

This is where artificial intelligence comes in. The early signals are many and varied: genes, the microbiome in the mouth, gut and lungs, immune cells, proteins in the blood, and environmental factors such as smoking. No single doctor can hold them all in mind, but machine-learning models can combine them into one picture, rank people by their likelihood of developing the disease, and perhaps identify who needs close follow-up and who does not.

Working on this is Fan Zhang, a machine-learning specialist, together with Kevin Deane, director of the Autoimmune Disease Prevention Center at the University of Colorado. The aim is not to diagnose the disease before it happens with certainty, but to build a tool that separates those at risk from the many people who carry antibodies and never develop the disease — because most antibody carriers never develop rheumatoid arthritis.

Another study: blood proteins predict the risk

In a parallel direction, the Journal of Translational Medicine published a study based on the UK Biobank, covering 47,537 people followed for about 14 years, in which the researchers measured 2,920 proteins in the blood. The team built a deep-learning model (DeepSurv) to predict the risk of developing rheumatoid arthritis, reaching an accuracy (C-index) of 0.786 — a good level for a prediction model.

Two proteins stood out: ICAM3, linked to higher risk (odds ratio 1.283), and IL18BP, linked to lower risk (odds ratio 0.635). These results do not mean a ready-made test is now available in laboratories, but they show that a protein signature in the blood may carry a predictive signal years before the disease.

What does this mean for patients today?

The most important message for patients is that preventing rheumatoid arthritis is still not routine clinical practice: there is no preventive drug prescribed to healthy people at risk, and no approved population screening for early detection. The tools the review discusses are still at the research and validation stage, and need larger trials before they reach clinics.

But the second message matters just as much: early diagnosis changes the course of the disease. The sooner treatment starts after symptoms appear, the less the joints are damaged. So if joint pain, swelling or morning stiffness lasts more than six weeks — especially with a family history of rheumatoid arthritis — a visit to the doctor should not be postponed, and asking for antibody and inflammation tests is a reasonable first step.

Tip

Do not wait for joint pain to “pass on its own” if it lasts more than six weeks, or if it comes with swelling or morning stiffness lasting more than half an hour: see your doctor, and mention any family history of rheumatoid arthritis. Smoking increases the risk of the disease and weakens the response to treatment, so quitting is a real preventive step. As for AI tests for early detection, they are still research tools — do not request them from a laboratory before medicine has adopted them.

The bottom line: an early window that could change the future of treatment

Rheumatoid arthritis begins years before the pain, and the body sends early signals that can be measured: antibodies, inflammation, immune changes and proteins in the blood. Artificial intelligence may combine these signals into an early screening tool, with promising accuracy in the first studies. But prevention is still not routine practice, and the tools are still research-stage. The hope is real, waiting is necessary, and the practical step today is early diagnosis once symptoms appear.
