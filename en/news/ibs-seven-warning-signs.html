Irritable bowel syndrome: 7 warning signs that call for a doctor’s visit

Share this article:

A stomach that has hurt for months… when is it IBS?

Abdominal pain that comes and goes, bloating after meals, diarrhoea or constipation or an alternation between the two, and a feeling that the bowel has not fully emptied. If you recognise these symptoms, you are not alone: irritable bowel syndrome affects about one person in ten worldwide, women about twice as often as men, and peaks between the ages of 20 and 45 — right in the middle of working and studying years.

In an opinion piece published by Al Jazeera on 22 November 2026, a professor of liver and digestive diseases explains that the syndrome, despite its distressing symptoms, is benign by nature: it does not turn into cancer, and it does not destroy bowel tissue. But he draws a clear line between the syndrome’s usual symptoms and seven warning signs that mean the diagnosis may be something else, and that a visit to the doctor has become necessary, not postponable.

What is irritable bowel syndrome?

IBS is a functional bowel disorder: the bowel is structurally sound, but its movement and sensitivity are disturbed, and the gut–brain axis — the constant dialogue between the nervous system and the digestive system — plays a part. That is why the syndrome so often goes with anxiety and depression: anxiety affects 39% of patients, depression 29%, and both together 23%, while fibromyalgia overlaps with it in 30% to 60% of cases.

The syndrome comes in four patterns: mixed (diarrhoea and constipation together) in 31–34%, constipation-predominant in 26–34%, diarrhoea-predominant in 26–28%, and unclassified in 4–8%. The most common symptoms are bloating in 66–80%, a feeling of incomplete emptying in 40–50%, mucus in the stool in about 30%, and upper digestive symptoms in 25–40%.

The seven signs that call for a doctor

These signs, called “red flags”, do not belong to the usual picture of IBS, and the presence of any one of them means the doctor should look for another cause, such as inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease (gluten sensitivity) or colorectal tumours:

bleeding from the rectum or black stools (melaena); unexplained weight loss of more than 5% within six months; waking from sleep because of pain or diarrhoea; iron-deficiency anaemia; first onset of symptoms after the age of 45–50; a family history of colorectal tumours, inflammatory bowel disease or coeliac disease; and raised inflammatory markers in blood tests.

One flag does not necessarily mean the diagnosis is serious, but it does mean that “IBS” should not be accepted as the diagnosis before other causes have been ruled out. Early diagnosis of conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease or colorectal tumours changes the outcome completely.

How is IBS diagnosed?

Diagnosis rests on the Rome criteria, which evolved from Rome III in 2006 to Rome IV in 2016, and are heading towards Rome V: recurrent abdominal pain or discomfort at least three days a month over three months, starting at least six months earlier, and related to defecation or to a change in stool frequency or form. There is no single test that proves IBS — the diagnosis is clinical, once the red flags have been ruled out.

One helpful test is faecal calprotectin: a value below 50 micrograms per gram rules out inflammatory bowel disease with an accuracy above 98%, a practical tool that separates a benign functional disorder from an organic inflammation that needs endoscopy and different treatment.

And what about treatment?

There is no single cure for IBS, but a plan that combines diet, medication and psychological support. The low-FODMAP diet — FODMAPs are short-chain sugars that ferment in the bowel — is followed for two to six weeks under specialist supervision, then foods are reintroduced gradually to identify triggers. Enteric-coated peppermint oil capsules help some patients, and cognitive behavioural therapy eases pain through the gut–brain axis.

Medicines include low doses of tricyclic antidepressants, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors or mirtazapine, which calm bowel sensitivity even without depression, as well as rifaximin, probiotics, tenapanor and linaclotide depending on the pattern. The reassuring message is that the syndrome does not turn into cancer and does not destroy the bowel, and that many people live a normal life with it under a suitable plan.

Tip

Keep a symptom diary for two weeks: what you ate, when the pain came, what the stool was like, whether you woke at night, and whether you lost weight. Take the diary to your doctor — it shortens the road to diagnosis. Do not start a low-FODMAP diet on your own for a long time: it is an elimination diet that needs specialist supervision and a planned reintroduction, so you do not deprive yourself of foods that do you no harm. And any one of the seven signs means: do not wait, see a doctor.

The bottom line: distressing but benign symptoms… except with these signs

IBS is common and distressing, but benign: it does not turn into cancer and does not destroy the bowel, it is diagnosed by the Rome criteria once other causes are ruled out, and it is managed with diet, medication and psychological support. But seven warning signs — bleeding, unexplained weight loss, waking at night, anaemia, symptom onset after 45, a family history, and raised inflammatory markers — change the whole story, and make a medical check a necessity, not an option.
