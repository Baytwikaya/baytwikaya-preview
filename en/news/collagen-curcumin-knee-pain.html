Collagen plus curcumin for knee pain: a small trial finds a benefit for athletes… not for osteoarthritis patients

Share this article:

A three-ingredient supplement… what did the trial find?

Many athletes suffer knee pain that appears during or after training, without having osteoarthritis in the joint. A new pilot trial from Nankai University in China, published in Nutrients on 14 November 2026, tested a supplement combining three ingredients — undenatured type II collagen, a bioavailability-enhanced curcumin, and hydrolysed bone powder — in 60 endurance athletes, and found that it improved their ability to tolerate load before reaching moderate pain, and eased their pain compared with placebo.

But before getting excited, it is essential to understand who the trial included and who it excluded: participants were adults aged 18 to 65, with a body mass index between 18.5 and 30, doing 3 or more endurance sessions a week for at least 3 months (running, swimming or cycling), with reversible load-dependent knee discomfort for at least 3 months — pain during or after exercise only, no pain at rest, no night pain, and no morning stiffness over 30 minutes. Anyone with diagnosed knee osteoarthritis, inflammatory joint disease, or a recent knee operation or injection was excluded. In other words, they were healthy athletes, not osteoarthritis patients.

What was in the capsule?

The researchers randomly divided participants into three groups of 20 people in a double-blind trial lasting 12 weeks, with 92% adherence and all 60 completing the twelve weeks. The first group took daily with meals 250 mg of curcumin phytosome (50 mg of curcumin), 800 mg of enzymatically hydrolysed bone powder (at least 60% chondroitin sulfate), and 40 mg of cartilage powder containing type II collagen protein (at least 4% undenatured type II collagen, about 1.6 mg).

The second group took the usual active comparator: glucosamine 1,500 mg and chondroitin 1,200 mg a day. The third group took a matched maltodextrin placebo. Participants kept their usual diet and exercise. The primary measure was the number of single-leg step-down repetitions until reaching moderate pain (level 5 out of 10); the researchers also measured repetitions until the first feeling of pain, knee range of motion, pain intensity, knee function on the WOMAC scale, and blood and urine biomarkers.

The numbers: 30 extra repetitions… and 1.6 points less pain

On the primary measure, the blend group managed 30.5 extra repetitions before reaching moderate pain compared with placebo after 12 weeks (95% CI: 8.7 to 52.3): within the group the count rose from 57.8 to 89.8 repetitions, while the placebo group rose by only 0.7 repetitions. The blend group also managed 21.4 extra repetitions before the first feeling of pain, and its pain on the visual pain scale fell by 1.6 points compared with placebo (from 4.9 to 2.1 within the group, versus 4.1 to 3.7 in the placebo group).

But the picture is completed by three observations. First, the blend group did not beat the glucosamine and chondroitin group on any direct comparison (a difference of 13.9 repetitions without significance, and 0.2 pain points without significance): the blend beat placebo, but did not prove superiority over the usual treatment. Second, the WOMAC daily-life function score showed no difference between the groups, nor did range of motion. Third, no biomarker reached a significant difference between the groups, and no adverse-event data appeared in the main text of the study despite being collected — so the supplement must not be called “safe” or “side-effect free”.

Why should the result be read with caution?

This is a small pilot trial: 20 people per group, with no formal sample-size calculation, and the authors describe it as hypothesis-generating, needing a larger confirmatory trial. The three-ingredient design without separate arms for each ingredient means the benefit cannot be credited to collagen or curcumin alone, nor can synergy be proven in humans (the synergy evidence cited is preclinical, in rats and cells).

The primary measure is also subjective and stops when the participant reports pain, so it can improve through pain modulation or familiarity with the task without any change in the joint itself, and the study included no imaging or structural measure. The duration was only 12 weeks, with no long-term data. There is no agreed minimal clinically important difference for repetitions-to-pain, so although the authors describe the effect as large (about 0.9 standard deviations), its clinical importance remains uncertain. Finally, the research was funded by the Nankai–MACTION Joint Laboratory of Nutrition Research, and the product manufacturer is MACTION — the paper disclosed this relationship, the authors declared no conflicts of interest, and stated that the funder and the company played no role in the design, analysis or publication.

What remains the proven treatment for knee pain?

For context, knee osteoarthritis is very common: it affects about 16% of people over 15 and 22.9% of those over 40 — about 654 million people over forty in 2020 — with women affected about 1.69 times more than men. The proven first-line treatment, under the guidelines of the American College of Rheumatology and the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, is patient education, structured exercise (strengthening plus aerobic exercise), and weight loss of at least 10% when overweight. Exercise reduces pain by about 12 points on a 100-point scale, according to a Cochrane review of 54 trials. Then come topical or oral anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroid injections for flares, and joint replacement when severe and other options have failed. No drug cures osteoarthritis, and the evidence for glucosamine and chondroitin is mixed and inconsistent.

So the new supplement replaces nothing: it is an early, promising signal for active people whose knees ache during or after training, not a treatment for knee osteoarthritis and not a reason to stop proven care. See a doctor if the pain is at rest or at night, or comes with swelling, locking, giving way, or after an injury. Note that curcumin can interact with blood thinners and other medicines, and that an off-the-shelf “collagen + curcumin” product will not match the tested formula (a very small undenatured collagen dose of about 1.6 mg, and a bioavailability-enhanced curcumin), so the results cannot be assumed to transfer to it. And do not attribute a percentage pain reduction to the study: the honest figure is a difference of 1.6 points on a 0–10 scale compared with placebo.

Tip

If you are an athlete whose knee aches during or after training, the news is promising but early: keep exercising and strengthening — that is the proven first-line treatment — and do not replace it with a supplement. If the pain is at rest or at night, or comes with swelling or locking, see a doctor before any supplement. Ask your doctor or pharmacist about curcumin interacting with your medicines, especially blood thinners, and do not buy a supplement on the basis that it is “the same formula” — the tested formula is specific and precise.

The bottom line: an early promising signal… not a treatment for osteoarthritis

The 2026 Nutrients pilot trial in 60 endurance athletes found that the collagen, curcumin and bone powder blend added about 30.5 repetitions before moderate pain compared with placebo, and eased pain by about 1.6 points on a 0–10 scale after 12 weeks. But it was a small trial with no sample-size calculation, it excluded osteoarthritis patients, it did not beat glucosamine and chondroitin, the daily-function score and the biomarkers showed no difference between the groups, and no adverse-event data were reported. It is a signal that deserves a bigger trial — not a treatment.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the studies

  • Publication: Gu et al., Nutrients 2026, vol. 18, issue 22, article 3785; published 14 November 2026; DOI: 10.3390/nu18223785; Nankai University, Tianjin, China; registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2600126913.
  • Design and sample: pilot randomised, double-blind, placebo- and active-comparator-controlled trial, 3 arms; 60 people (20 per group), all completing 12 weeks; adults 18–65, body mass index 18.5–30, doing at least 3 endurance sessions a week, with activity-related knee pain only and no pain at rest; knee osteoarthritis patients excluded.
  • Formula: group A: 250 mg curcumin phytosome (50 mg curcumin) + 800 mg hydrolysed bone powder (at least 60% chondroitin sulfate) + 40 mg cartilage powder (about 1.6 mg undenatured collagen) daily with meals; group B: glucosamine 1,500 mg + chondroitin 1,200 mg; group C: placebo.
  • Primary measure: single-leg step-down repetitions to moderate pain (level 5 of 10): +30.5 repetitions versus placebo after 12 weeks (95% CI: 8.7–52.3; p = 0.0201); within the group from 57.8 to 89.8 repetitions, versus +0.7 in the placebo group.
  • Pain: visual pain scale fell by 1.6 points versus placebo (CI: −2.6 to −0.7; p = 0.0030); within the group from 4.9 to 2.1; repetitions to first pain +21.4 (p = 0.0037).
  • Head-to-head: no significant difference between the blend and glucosamine/chondroitin (13.9 repetitions, p = 0.6144; 0.2 pain points); no between-group difference in WOMAC daily function, range of motion or any biomarker.
  • Limitations: small pilot trial (20 per group) with no sample-size calculation; the effect cannot be credited to one ingredient; subjective pain-limited measure; 12 weeks only; no adverse-event data in the main text; funded by the Nankai–MACTION joint laboratory (manufacturer MACTION), with disclosure and no declared conflicts of interest.
  • Context: knee osteoarthritis affects about 16% of people over 15 and 22.9% of those over 40 (about 654 million people over forty in 2020); the proven first-line treatment is education, structured exercise and weight loss of at least 10% when overweight; exercise reduces pain by about 12 points on a 100-point scale (Cochrane, 54 trials).
