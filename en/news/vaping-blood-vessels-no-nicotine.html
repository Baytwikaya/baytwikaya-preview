One vaping session harms blood vessels… even without nicotine

Share this article:

No nicotine… yet the blood vessels are harmed at once

Many people say a nicotine-free e-cigarette is “safe”, because nicotine is the harmful substance. But new preliminary findings from an MRI study challenge that assumption: a single vaping session changed blood vessel function at once, even when the liquid contained no nicotine at all. More striking still, vaping affected a greater number of markers than an ordinary cigarette did.

The study was led by Marianne Nabbout, a radiology resident at the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences, and was conducted at the University of Pennsylvania; its results will be presented on 2 December 2026 at the annual meeting of the Radiological Society of North America. It is important to understand the nature of these findings from the start: they are preliminary conference results, not yet published in a peer-reviewed journal, and not yet peer-reviewed. They are a strong signal, not a final verdict.

What did the researchers do? Three sessions, with an MRI before and after each

The study included 41 healthy adult smokers and vapers, aged 20 to 50 years with a mean age of 29.8, all of whom had smoked or vaped daily for more than a year. It also included a comparison group of 17 people aged 21 to 33 who had never smoked or vaped.

Each participant went through three separate sessions, and in each session had an MRI scan before and after the exposure: once with a tobacco cigarette, once with an e-cigarette containing nicotine, and once with a nicotine-free e-cigarette. The team measured how quickly blood flow and oxygen recovered after a brief restriction, the stiffness of the aorta, how well brain blood vessels responded to changing demands, the brain’s oxygen use, and the flexibility of the carotid artery.

What did they find? Oxygen saturation fell after every exposure

The headline result is that venous oxygen saturation — the oxygen saturation measured in a vein of the thigh in this study — fell sharply at once after each of the three exposures: vaping with nicotine, vaping without nicotine, and ordinary smoking. Blood flow and oxygen measures changed after a single session, including a session with nicotine-free liquid, suggesting that the immediate effect on the circulation is not due to nicotine alone.

The second surprise is that the vaping challenge harmed a greater number of blood-vessel function markers than the smoking challenge did. Nabbout said the study shows that a single vaping session produces a measurable effect on markers of vascular function, and that substances other than nicotine in the vape aerosol may be responsible for part of the harm. But the team stressed that the study looked only at immediate effects, and that longer-term studies are needed to know whether these temporary changes contribute to lasting health problems.

Not the first time: a 2019 study found the same thing in people who had never smoked

These findings are supported by an older study published in the journal Radiology in 2019, funded by the US National Institutes of Health: it enrolled 31 people in their twenties and early thirties who had never smoked a traditional or electronic cigarette, and each took just 16 puffs from a nicotine-free e-cigarette. One hour later, MRI showed reduced blood flow in the femoral artery compared with the normal state before vaping. That study concluded that repeated vaping could cause cumulative damage to the blood vessels and raise the risk of heart disease.

The difference between the two studies is that the 2019 study enrolled people who had never smoked and used a small, fixed dose, while the 2026 study enrolled daily smokers and vapers and compared vaping with smoking directly in the same people. Even so, the most important question remains unanswered in both studies: do these immediate changes turn into real cardiovascular disease over time? Neither study proves that, and neither rules it out.

Tip

There is no “safe” vape: even nicotine-free liquid changed blood vessel function at once in this study, and vaping is not a healthy alternative to smoking but another product with its own risks. If you use vaping to quit smoking, talk to your doctor about proven quitting aids, and do not treat “nicotine-free” as a safety certificate — the findings are preliminary and the long-term effects are still being studied.

The bottom line: one session is enough to change the blood vessels… and nicotine is not the whole story

Preliminary findings from an MRI study (University of Pennsylvania, led by Marianne Nabbout) to be presented at the RSNA meeting on 2 December 2026: a single vaping session changed blood flow and oxygen saturation at once, even with nicotine-free liquid; venous oxygen saturation fell after every exposure, and vaping affected more markers than an ordinary cigarette. The findings are preliminary and not yet peer-reviewed, and they measure immediate effects only; a 2019 study in Radiology found a similar effect in people who had never smoked. The message: “nicotine-free” does not mean “harm-free”.

Key facts: the essential numbers from the studies

  • The study: preliminary conference findings, led by Marianne Nabbout (University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences), conducted at the University of Pennsylvania; to be presented on 2 December 2026 at the annual meeting of the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA); funded by the US National Institutes of Health (grant R01 HL155243); not yet published in a peer-reviewed journal.
  • Sample: 41 healthy adult smokers and vapers, aged 20–50 years with a mean age of 29.8, smoking or vaping daily for more than a year; and a comparison group of 17 people aged 21–33 who had never smoked or vaped.
  • Design: three separate sessions per participant, with an MRI scan before and after each exposure: a tobacco cigarette, an e-cigarette with nicotine, and a nicotine-free e-cigarette.
  • Headline result: venous oxygen saturation in the femoral vein fell sharply at once after each of the three exposures, including the nicotine-free liquid; blood flow and oxygen measures changed after a single session.
  • Vaping versus smoking: the vaping challenge harmed a greater number of blood-vessel function markers than the smoking challenge did.
  • What was measured: how quickly blood flow and oxygen recovered after a brief restriction, aortic stiffness, brain blood-vessel responsiveness, brain oxygen use, and carotid artery flexibility.
  • Limits: the findings are preliminary and not peer-reviewed; they measure immediate effects only; participants were already daily smokers and vapers; the question of long-term effects remains unanswered.
  • For comparison (2019): 31 people who had never smoked, just 16 puffs from a nicotine-free e-cigarette; one hour later, blood flow in the femoral artery had fallen (Radiology journal).
