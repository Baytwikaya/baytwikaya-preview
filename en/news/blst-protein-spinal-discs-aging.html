A hidden protein protects the spinal discs from ageing

Share this article:

A protein that was unknown… and is now a promising name in back pain

Why do the spinal discs wear out with age in some people more than in others? A new study from the University of Utah, published in the journal Aging Cell on 9 October 2026, opens an unexpected door: the researchers identified for the first time the role of a protein called BLST, the short name for the zinc finger protein ZNF865, in protecting spinal discs from ageing. The study was led by Robby D. Bowles, of the University of Utah’s Department of Orthopaedics, in collaboration with the Huntsman Cancer Institute.

The discs are the cartilage cushions between the vertebrae of the spine, and their degeneration with age is a common cause of chronic back pain. When the cells of the disc nucleus age, they stop repairing the matrix that builds the disc and start releasing inflammatory signals known as the “senescence-associated secretory phenotype”, so the structure deteriorates and pain appears. What guards these cells? The answer, the study suggests, may be this protein.

First: they found the protein fades with age and disease

The team began by comparing human and mouse disc samples, and found that BLST expression falls with advancing age and with pathological degeneration, in both human and mouse samples. BLST is a zinc finger protein made of 1,059 amino acids, and until now it was almost entirely uncharacterised functionally: this study is the first functional characterisation of this gene and protein.

To confirm that the relationship was causal and not just an association, the researchers used a CRISPR tool to restore BLST expression in human disc cells taken from the disc nucleus. What happened? Cellular senescence fell, expression of the inflammatory secretory pattern fell, DNA damage lessened, extracellular matrix building was enhanced, and chromatin accessibility and gene expression returned to their healthy state. In other words, restoring the protein brought the cell back to its healthy state.

Second: switching the protein off in animals caused degeneration and pain

The team then tested the opposite direction in animals: they used a CRISPRi tool to switch off BLST expression in rodent discs, and the discs degenerated while pain-related behaviours appeared. The picture is now complete in both directions: losing the protein causes the disease, and restoring it restores health.

It is worth noting that BLST is widely expressed across different tissues of the body, not only in the discs, which means it may have broader roles in repairing DNA damage and cellular senescence, and may also be relevant to cancer, as Jason Gertz of the Huntsman Cancer Institute, a co-author of the study, noted. The protein may be a general guardian of DNA integrity, not just a disc-specific one.

When might this become a treatment? The road is still long

It is very important to put this discovery in its proper scale: it is laboratory and animal work, not a human treatment. There is no drug targeting BLST yet, no clinical trial in humans, and CRISPR-based therapy remains a future direction, not an available option. What the discovery offers is a new targetable goal and a new way of understanding disc degeneration, not a promise of a treatment soon.

Even so, the value of the discovery is great: back pain is one of the most common causes of chronic pain and disability in the world, and current treatments mostly manage the pain rather than treating the disc degeneration itself. If researchers later succeed in safely restoring BLST activity in human discs, that could become the first treatment that addresses the cause of the degeneration rather than its symptoms. That is what makes the hidden protein a story worth following, even if the road to the clinic is long.

Tip

There is no BLST-based treatment yet, so do not wait for a drug soon and do not buy any product claiming to target this protein. What protects your back today is proven: regular movement, strengthening the trunk and back muscles, good posture, avoiding long sitting, and seeing a doctor for persistent pain, numbness or weakness in the legs. The discovery is promising, but daily prevention is what you have now.

The bottom line: a hidden protein guards the discs… and its treatment is still in the laboratory

The 2026 University of Utah study in Aging Cell identified for the first time the role of the BLST protein (ZNF865) in spinal discs: its expression falls with age and disease in human and mouse samples; restoring it with a CRISPR tool in human cells reduced cellular senescence, DNA damage and the inflammatory secretory pattern and enhanced matrix building, while switching it off in rodents caused disc degeneration and pain. The discovery is laboratory and animal work only; there is no drug or human trial yet; its value is that it is a new therapeutic target for understanding back pain and treating it at its root.
