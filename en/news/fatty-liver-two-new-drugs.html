Fatty liver: two new drugs open the door to a treatment that did not exist

Share this article:

Fatty liver had no drug… until now

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, shortened to MASLD, is fat building up in the liver beyond 5% together with at least one metabolic risk factor, such as obesity, diabetes or high lipids. When the fat comes with inflammation and cell injury, it becomes metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, or MASH, which can progress to fibrosis, then cirrhosis and liver cancer.

For many years the only treatment was weight loss and lifestyle change, with no approved drug targeting the liver itself. Today, new European guidelines issued in September 2026 by the European associations for the liver, diabetes and obesity (EASL/EASD/EASO) recommend two specific drugs for patients with MASH and moderate or advanced fibrosis — alongside diet and exercise, not instead of them.

The first drug: resmetirom

Resmetirom (Rezdiffra) is a daily tablet that works as a selective agonist of the thyroid hormone receptor beta in the liver, speeding up fat burning there. The US Food and Drug Administration approved it in March 2024 under accelerated approval, for adults with MASH and stage F2–F3 fibrosis, without cirrhosis.

In the main trial, which included 966 patients, 26–30% of patients achieved resolution of the inflammation without worsening of fibrosis after 52 weeks, compared with about 10% in the placebo group, and fibrosis improved by at least one stage in 24–26% versus 14%. Its side effects include diarrhoea, nausea, gallbladder and liver problems, and interactions with statin drugs, which is why it needs close medical follow-up.

The second drug: semaglutide

Semaglutide (Wegovy) is a weekly injection from the GLP-1 receptor agonist family, known for treating diabetes and obesity. The US Food and Drug Administration approved it in August 2025 for the same use: MASH with F2–F3 fibrosis, without cirrhosis.

In the interim analysis of the ESSENCE trial, which included 800 patients, 62.9% of patients achieved resolution of the inflammation without worsening of fibrosis after 72 weeks, compared with 34.3% in the placebo group, and fibrosis improved in 36.8% versus 22.4%, with an average weight loss of 10.5%. Its side effects include nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and constipation, with a risk of pancreatitis and gallbladder problems.

Which one suits whom?

The two drugs have not been compared directly in a single trial, and they are not routinely used together. The guidelines suggest semaglutide first for people who have obesity, diabetes or a high cardiovascular risk, because it treats those problems together with the liver. Resmetirom is suggested for people who are not obese, or who cannot tolerate GLP-1 drugs.

In both cases, the drug remains an addition to lifestyle, not a replacement for it, and it is prescribed only to people whose fibrosis has been confirmed by tests such as the FIB-4 index and FibroScan — not to everyone who has fat in the liver. Most people with fatty liver do not need a drug at all, but weight loss and a lifestyle change.

The foundation is still diet and movement

Even with the two new drugs, the most important numbers remain the weight-loss numbers: losing 5% of body weight reduces liver fat, losing 7–10% reduces inflammation, and losing more than 10% improves the chances of fibrosis getting better. The best-evidenced dietary pattern is the Mediterranean diet, together with at least 150 minutes of physical activity a week.

As for the other drugs under testing — such as tirzepatide, efruxifermin, lanifibranor and survodutide — none had received US Food and Drug Administration approval for treating MASH as of November 2026, and they remain within clinical trials.

Tip

If your doctor tells you that you have fat in your liver, ask about the degree of fibrosis: that is what decides whether you need a drug or not, using simple tests such as FIB-4 and FibroScan. And start with what is certain: lose 5% of your weight first, cut back on sugars and sweetened drinks, and walk 150 minutes a week. Do not request the two drugs on your own and do not buy them online: they are prescription-only, each has its side effects, and they are not routinely used together.

The bottom line: two new drugs, but lifestyle first

For the first time, inflamed fatty liver with fibrosis has an approved drug treatment: resmetirom (a daily tablet) and semaglutide (a weekly injection), both of which showed improvements in inflammation and fibrosis in trials, and both recommended by the new European guidelines. But the drugs are only for patients with confirmed fibrosis, and alongside diet and exercise, not instead of them, and they are not used together. The news opens a door that was closed — but the first door is still weight loss.

Key facts: the essential numbers

  • Definitions: MASLD is fat in the liver above 5% with at least one metabolic risk factor; MASH is fat with inflammation and cell injury; fibrosis is graded from F0 to F4.
  • Guidelines: EASL/EASD/EASO, September 2026 — two drugs recommended for patients with MASH and F2–F3 fibrosis, alongside diet and exercise.
  • Resmetirom (Rezdiffra): a daily tablet, a selective thyroid hormone receptor beta agonist; US FDA approval March 2024 (accelerated approval); trial of 966 patients.
  • Resmetirom results after 52 weeks: inflammation resolved without worsening fibrosis in 26–30% versus about 10% on placebo; fibrosis improved by at least one stage in 24–26% versus 14%.
  • Semaglutide (Wegovy): a weekly injection from the GLP-1 family; US FDA approval August 2025; ESSENCE interim trial of 800 patients.
  • Semaglutide results after 72 weeks: inflammation resolved without worsening fibrosis in 62.9% versus 34.3% on placebo; fibrosis improved in 36.8% versus 22.4%; average weight loss 10.5%.
  • Side effects: resmetirom — diarrhoea, nausea, gallbladder and liver problems, and statin interactions; semaglutide — nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, constipation, and a risk of pancreatitis and gallbladder problems.
  • Weight loss: losing 5% reduces liver fat; 7–10% reduces inflammation; more than 10% improves the chances of fibrosis improving; Mediterranean diet, and at least 150 minutes of physical activity a week.
  • Drugs under testing and not approved for treating MASH as of November 2026: tirzepatide, efruxifermin, lanifibranor and survodutide.
